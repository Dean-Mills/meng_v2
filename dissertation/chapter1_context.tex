\section{Context}
\label{sec_intro_context}

Multi-person pose estimation is the task of identifying and locating a fixed set of anatomical keypoints -
the seventeen joints of the COCO skeleton~\cite{lin2014coco} - on every person present in an image. The 
bottom-up family of methods breaks the task up into two distinct stages: a detector first identifies
every keypoint in the image (regardless of which person it belongs to) and then the grouping stage
assigns each detected keypoint to a person (Figure~\ref{fig:context_example}). This separation of concerns is what
defines the bottom-up methods starting from the smallest unit available (a joint) and working 
up towards a person. This allows the runtime to be approximately constant in the number of people
but means that the grouping decision itself becomes difficult.

    \centering

    which is what the grouping stage is given;

    In (b) the same keypoints are coloured by the person they belong to and joined by the COCO skeleton,
    which is what the grouping stage has to produce.
    The two people on the right overlap while the person on the left does not.}
    \label{fig:context_example}
\end{figure}

In this context multi-person pose estimation is then two things: keypoint detection and keypoint
grouping. The detection stage is well studied with high-resolution 
convolutional backbones~\cite{sun2019hrnet, cheng2020higherhrnet}, transformer estimators~\cite{xu2022vitpose},
and human-centric foundation models~\cite{khirodkar2024sapiens} which 
have pushed per-keypoint localisation accuracy on the COCO benchmark toward saturation. Residual errors
of modern bottom-up pipelines happen downstream of detection in deciding which person each 
detected keypoint belongs to. This decision is quite difficult for
a number of structural reasons:

\begin{compactitem}
    \item The number of people in the scene is unknown at inference time.
    \item Every person can contribute at most one keypoint of each joint type.
    \item Occlusion and truncation remove keypoints unpredictably.
    \item People who overlap in the image (without occluding) place same-type keypoints
    close to each other, for example two left elbows.
\end{compactitem}

In established bottom-up methods, this grouping decision is produced by a mechanism
that is trained jointly with the keypoint detector. Part-affinity fields~\cite{cao2017openpose},
associative-embedding tags~\cite{newell2017associative, cheng2020higherhrnet},
centre-offset regression~\cite{geng2021dekr},
and end-to-end set prediction~\cite{shi2022petr} all couple the grouping method 
to a particular estimation backbone. This coupling buys accuracy 
at the cost of reusability - a grouping mechanism trained with respect to one detector 
cannot be detached and applied to the output of another one. This work studies the alternative:
a grouping module that consumes arbitrary detector-emitted keypoints and types. The question is 
what that costs in accuracy and whether the modularity is worth it.
